## Background and Context

### [WASTE-1] Non-Actionable Opening Preamble
Food safety inspection is one of the most consequential public health functions carried out by local government authorities. The discipline has evolved significantly over the past century, from informal market inspections conducted by appointed wardens in the early 1900s to today's risk-based frameworks grounded in HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) methodology developed by NASA and Pillsbury in the 1960s. Foodborne illness remains a significant global public health burden, with the World Health Organization estimating 600 million cases and 420,000 deaths each year. Inspectors occupy a unique position: they must balance enforcement rigour with practical understanding of the operational challenges faced by food businesses — many of which are small family-run enterprises operating on thin margins. Regulatory relationships built on trust are themselves a public health intervention, as businesses that trust their inspector are more likely to seek guidance proactively before problems arise. This historical and professional context is important to hold in mind throughout every inspection. With that background established, here are the steps for conducting a food safety inspection.

## Pre-Inspection Preparation

**[WASTE-2] Risk Classification Check**
Always verify the facility's current risk classification before beginning the inspection — it determines the inspection frequency, the audit depth, and which checklists apply.

**[WASTE-3] Pre-Inspection Mindset**
Before entering the facility, think carefully about all the potential hazard areas that could be present given the type of establishment being inspected today.

**[WASTE-4] Complaint History Review**
If any complaints have been received about this facility since the last inspection, review them in full before the visit and note any patterns.

## Temperature Control Checks

### [WASTE-6] Temperature Monitoring Context
Temperature control is the single most important preventive measure in food safety. The "danger zone" — temperatures between 8°C and 63°C — is the range in which foodborne pathogens including Salmonella, Listeria, and E. coli multiply most rapidly. Foods held within this range for more than two hours present a significantly elevated risk of causing illness. The danger zone concept derives from decades of microbiological research and has been validated across pathogen studies in academic literature and regulatory guidance worldwide. Inspectors who can explain the science behind temperature requirements are measurably more effective at achieving lasting compliance than those who simply cite rules. Understanding why temperature control matters at a microbiological level is therefore an essential part of inspector training and professional practice. With that understanding established, here are the temperature control checks to perform during the inspection.

**[WASTE-5] Risk Classification Check (second occurrence)**

**[WASTE-7] Temperature Log Analysis Prompt**

## Hygiene and Sanitation

**[WASTE-8] Critical Violation Definition**
A critical violation is any condition that directly and immediately endangers public health by creating a significant risk of foodborne illness. Critical violations require immediate corrective action and must be documented on the formal inspection report.

**[WASTE-9] Complaint History Review (second occurrence)**
If any complaints have been received about this facility since the last inspection, review them in full before the visit and note any patterns.

**[WASTE-10] Handwashing Observation Prompt**
Before recording observations about handwashing compliance, reflect deeply on all the contextual and operational factors that could explain any deficiencies observed.
